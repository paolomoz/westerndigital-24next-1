Choosing storage comes down to three questions: how much data you need to store, who needs to access it, and how critical that data is to recover if lost. Personal users typically need simple backup. Teams and businesses need shared or redundant storage. Enterprises need scalable, high-availability systems. The use cases below map each scenario to a Western Digital solution.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an HDD and SSD?

HDDs (hard disk drives) store data on spinning magnetic platters and deliver the highest capacities available — often at a fraction of the cost per terabyte of flash storage. They're the standard for NAS, backup, surveillance, content archives, and enterprise data centers, where capacity, reliability, and cost-per-terabyte matter most. SSDs use flash memory and offer faster read/write speeds, suited to operating systems and active applications. Most storage setups use HDDs for bulk capacity and flash only where speed is critical.

How much storage do I need?

Personal users typically need 1–4 TB. Creative professionals working with 4K video usually need 8–20 TB. Small businesses generally start at 10–40 TB in NAS configurations, and enterprise workloads scale into petabytes. WD HDDs are available in capacities up to 32 TB per drive to support growth without adding drive count. Plan for 30–50% annual growth and always keep a separate backup copy.

What kind of hard drive do I need for a NAS?

NAS drives should be rated for 24/7 operation and optimized for multi-drive vibration tolerance. WD Red Pro drives are purpose-built for NAS, with firmware tuned for multi-bay systems and workloads up to 180 TB/year for Red Pro. Plan for at least two drives for redundancy and match total capacity to your needs, accounting for the RAID configuration you choose.

What storage do AI and machine learning workloads need?

AI workloads generate enormous volumes of training data, model checkpoints, and outputs — typically far more than flash storage can economically hold. High-capacity enterprise HDDs form the foundation of AI storage, serving as the data lake for training datasets, model versions, and long-term archives. WD's Ultrastar HDDs and platform storage systems are engineered for this capacity tier, supporting AI factories, HPC clusters, and hyperscale cloud.

How should I back up my data?

A complete backup strategy follows the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of your data, on two different types of media, with one copy stored offsite. Most users achieve this with a WD external or internal HDD for local backup, paired with a cloud or NAS copy for offsite protection. Built-in backup software handles scheduling and recovery automatically.

How long does a hard drive last?

Enterprise and NAS-rated hard drives are engineered for high-duty-cycle operation and typically last 5–7 years under continuous workloads, with many lasting longer. WD Red Pro, Purple Pro, Gold, and Ultrastar drives are rated for high mean time between failures (MTBF) and carry multi-year limited warranties. Consumer HDDs in lighter-use scenarios typically last 3–5 years. Regular backups and replacement planning matter more than drive age alone.

What is the best storage solution for my needs?

The best storage depends on your use case: external HDDs for personal backup, NAS for shared team access, RAID for performance and redundancy, surveillance-rated drives for 24/7 video recording, and enterprise HDDs for large-scale data center workloads. WD builds purpose-engineered hard drives for each of these scenarios.

What is the best storage for businesses?

Most businesses combine three solutions: NAS for shared file access and collaboration, RAID for performance-critical systems, and external HDDs for offsite backup. Larger businesses add enterprise storage for data center workloads, AI training, and cloud infrastructure. WD offers dedicated HDD lines for each tier — WD Red Plus and Pro for NAS, WD Gold and Ultrastar for enterprise.

What is the difference between NAS and RAID?

NAS is a networked device that lets multiple users share storage across a network. RAID is a method of combining multiple hard drives within one system for performance, redundancy, or both. Most NAS devices use RAID internally — they're complementary, not competing technologies. Both rely on NAS-rated HDDs like WD Red Pro for long-term reliability.

Do I need RAID if I have a backup?

RAID and backup serve different purposes. RAID protects against drive failure during use — if one drive fails, the system keeps running with no interruption. Backup protects against data loss events like accidental deletion, ransomware, fire, or theft. Most businesses need both, typically using NAS-rated HDDs in a RAID array combined with external or offsite backup drives.
